# Culturally Specific Theory — Anchored Cases Only

Source: `civilization_design_music_research.md` Part II §14 (2026-11-21). Each case names region, community, and sources; each carries its **required critique**. Claims outside these anchored cases must build an equivalent anchored case (geography/date/community/evidence tier) **before** any claim is made. Fail-stop when a culturally specific claim cannot be tied to a tradition-internal or peer-reviewed ethnomusicological source.

**No homogenization.** These traditions are not interchangeable "world music theory." Do not merge systems, do not map them onto Western categories without saying that is what you are doing, and never present a 20th-century codification as ancient doctrine.

## 1. Hindustani raga/tala — North India (not "Indian music")

- **Tradition-internal sources:** Vishnu Narayan Bhatkhande's early-20th-century systematization (*Hindustani Sangeet Paddhati*, 1910–1932; thath-based classification) — a **modern codification, not ancient doctrine**; N. A. Jairazbhoy, *The Rāgs of North Indian Music: Their Structure and Evolution* (1971/1995).
- **Required critique:** Janaki Bakhle, *Two Men and Music: Nationalism in the Making of an Indian Classical Tradition* (Oxford, 2005) documents the colonial-era/nationalist reframing of "classical" — **required reading before any pan-Indian claim.**
- **Tala:** Hindustani tala cycles (e.g., tintal, 16-beat) with theka practice; distinguish from the Carnatic suladi sapta tala system (7 talas × 5 jatis) — different systems, different regions.

## 2. Carnatic theory — South India

- P. Sambamoorthy, *South Indian Music* (6 vols.); the 72-melakarta system codified by Venkatamakhin (*Chaturdandi Prakasika*, c. 17th c., Thanjavur court context).
- Raga as **melodic grammar** (arohana/avarohana plus characteristic phrases), not a scale; performance practice differs from text lists — say so.

## 3. Maqam — Arab/Ottoman/Persian spheres (do not merge)

- **Historical theory:** al-Farabi, *Kitab al-Musiqi al-Kabir* (10th c.); Safi al-Din al-Urmawi, *Kitab al-Adwar* (13th c., Baghdad) — the first systematic modal treatise with letter notation.
- **Modern practice:** Scott Marcus, *Music in Egypt: Experiencing Music, Expressing Culture* (Oxford, 2007); Ali Jihad Racy, *Making Music in the Arab World: The Culture and Artistry of Tarab* (Cambridge, 2003) — maqam as jins (tetrachord/trichord) composition with regionally variable intonation.
- **Required critique:** the 1932 Cairo Congress quarter-tone standardization was contested then and now — present it as a **modernization project, not the tradition**.
- **Intonation honesty:** measured practice varies by performer/region; do not assert a single "maqam tuning."

## 4. Gamelan — Java and Bali are different; name which

- Jaap Kunst, *Music in Java* (3rd ed., 1973); Sumarsam, *Gamelan: Cultural Interaction and Musical Development in Central Java* (Chicago, 1995); Michael Tenzer, *Balinese Music* (1991) and *Gamelan Gong Kebyar* (2000).
- **Critical fact:** slendro and pelog are interval **categories**, and each gamelan set has its own tuning (embuh) — there is no standard slendro tuning. Colotomic structure (gong-cycle punctuation) is the documented organizing principle.
- **Banned on sight:** "gamelan uses equal-tempered pentatonic."

## 5. West African rhythm — named peoples/repertoires only

- V. Kofi Agawu, *African Rhythm: A Northern Ewe Perspective* (Cambridge, 1995) — both the canonical Northern Ewe (Ghana) account **and** a critique of homogenizing "African rhythm" discourse.
- David Locke, *Drum Gahu: An Introduction to African Rhythm* (1998) — documented Ewe dance-drumming repertoire.
- Simha Arom, *African Polyphony and Polyrhythm* (Cambridge, 1991) — Central African (Aka) polyphony with transcription method.
- Gerhard Kubik, *Theory of African Music* (2 vols., 2010).
- **Required critique:** Agawu, "The Invention of 'African Rhythm'," *JAMS* 48(3) (1995): 380–395. "West African rhythm is polymetric" is a homogenizing summary and fails review; teach specific timelines (e.g., the gankogui bell pattern of Gahu) with repertoire and community named.

## Euclidean-rhythm attribution discipline (cross-reference)

Toussaint's E(k,n) maximally-even patterns (see theory_foundations.md §3) are a **modern analytical method** (Bjorklund 2003, Toussaint 2005/2019). When a generated pattern matches a documented timeline (e.g., tresillo E(3,8)), the cultural attribution must cite the ethnomusicological source at page level — never present the algorithm as evidence that "all cultures share one rhythm."
